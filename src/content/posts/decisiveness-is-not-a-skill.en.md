A WeChat piece going around Chinese consulting circles makes a clean argument. The title carries the whole thesis: 《咨询顾问的核心竞争力，从"好品味"走向"能决断"》 — the consultant's core edge moving from good taste to the nerve to decide. Tools can teach you taste now, it says, so the scarce person is the one who'll make the call and put their name on it. Taste is the radar. Decisiveness is pressing the button.

I agree with the direction. The words are wrong, in a way that matters.

## Its "taste" is my "judgment"

Look at the examples it uses. Knowing which analysis this board actually cares about. Spotting the recommendation that's technically right and dead on arrival. Knowing which slide to cut. Every one of those reads a standard that already exists. The board holds it, and the senior person learned to predict it.

That's judgment, as I've defined it before: applying a standard that's already there, faster and more accurately than anyone else in the room. Taste, I've argued, is a different animal. It's a bet on a standard that hasn't formed yet, with no wrong answer at the moment you make it.

That category never shows up in the piece. It doesn't read as a disagreement. It reads as a missing slot.

## "Decisiveness" is two things wearing one word

One is saying the sentence. Here's the path we recommend. The other is carrying what comes after it: your name, your reputation, your next review.

A model can already write the first one. It'll never supply the second, and not because of anything about intelligence. It has nothing to lose. I wrote a version of this in the barbell piece — AI can produce a logically airtight recommendation and bear no consequence for it.

So the arc isn't taste to decisiveness. It's capability to accountability.

## Which makes it a pricing problem

Here's where I'd push hardest. The piece treats the willingness to decide as a personal quality. Some people have the nerve, most don't, and firms should make room for the ones who do. I think most of what it's describing is the payoff structure.

Get it right and it was obvious. Get it wrong and you own it for years. Under that asymmetry the rational move is advice that can't be wrong and can't change anything, which is the exact failure the piece names in its own section on firm culture. It sees the symptom and files it under culture.

If the shortage is incentives and not nerve, courage training is the wrong medicine. The medicine is a contract. Outcome underwriting is already that at the firm level, a way to make the signature bind. The individual version barely exists.

## What would settle it

Watch whether outcome-linked pay reaches individuals. Every number we have so far is a firm number. I cited one in [consulting-outcomes](/posts/consulting-outcomes): McKinsey's Bob Sternfels telling HBR that about a third of revenues are underwriting outcomes. That's the firm's revenue at risk, not any partner's own pay. I haven't seen the individual version anywhere. If by 2028 a meaningful share of a senior consultant's personal pay rides on a call they made themselves, the signature is being priced and the shortage was structural. If outcome risk stays on the firm's books and individual pay is set the way it is now, I'm wrong and the original piece is closer to right. It really was about who has the nerve.

The honest counterpoint: two people under identical incentives don't behave the same, and I can't explain that gap away. I also can't account for the person who signs their name when nothing rewards it. That person exists. They're probably the one both of us are actually writing about.
